01Why it sells

In the Insurance Authority's provisional statistics for 2025, long-term new business premiums (excluding retirement schemes) rose 50.6% in a year to HK$330.9bn. Individual non-linked participating business accounted for HK$282.8bn (+55.1%), or 85.5% of the total.[1][2]

Participating policies let you build up long-term assets in US or Hong Kong dollars while carrying life cover, so they are widely used as a savings tool. Demand is also supported by the fact that Hong Kong insurers publish fulfilment ratios for participating products (bonuses actually paid compared with past illustrations), so buyers can compare companies' track records before choosing.

02How it works

STEP 1
Paying premiums
Single premiums or short premium terms of a few years are common. Policies are usually in US or Hong Kong dollars.
STEP 2
The participating fund
Premiums are pooled in the insurer's participating fund (par fund) and invested in bonds, equities and other assets.
STEP 3
Bonus declarations
Depending on investment performance, the insurer sets bonuses that build up each year and bonuses settled on surrender or maturity. Neither is guaranteed.
STEP 4
Payout or cover
On surrender you receive the guaranteed surrender value plus bonuses; if the insured person dies, the death benefit is paid.

03Key features

Two layers: guaranteed and non-guaranteed

Non-guaranteed bonuses sit on top of a guaranteed surrender value. A large part of the long-term return comes from the non-guaranteed part.

Built for the long term

If you surrender early, you typically get back less than you paid in. Plan how long you will keep the policy first.

Policy succession features

Depending on the plan, features such as changing the insured person or splitting the policy are used to pass assets on within a family.

04Risks and points to note

  • Bonuses are not promisesThe bonuses in the illustration you receive when you apply are figures based on assumptions. If investment performance is poor, actual bonuses will be lower.
  • Losses on early surrenderThe earlier you surrender, the more likely you are to get back less than you paid. Consider how much you can afford to pay and how long you will keep the policy.
  • Exchange ratesThe value in won depends on the won–dollar exchange rate. The Hong Kong dollar is pegged to the US dollar, so these are in effect dollar assets.
  • Insurer credit and governing lawThe ability to pay depends on the insurer's financial strength, and the contract is governed by Hong Kong law.

05Checkpoints for Korean residents

  • Limits on offering and arranging it in KoreaBeing offered this product in Korea, or buying it through an intermediary, is restricted in principle. The exceptions are narrowly defined in the Enforcement Decree.Korea's Insurance Business Act, Article 3
  • Applying in Hong KongHong Kong insurers usually take applications and signatures in Hong Kong itself. Each insurer has its own rules on accepting non-residents.
  • Remittance and foreign exchangeWhen you send premiums abroad, your bank will check the purpose of the transaction and supporting documents. Ask your bank first whether a foreign exchange report is required.
  • Tax and reportingCheck with a tax adviser how surrender payouts and bonuses are taxed in Korea and whether the policy must be reported as an overseas financial account.National Tax Service · Overseas financial account reporting
  • Tax on insurance gainsIf what you receive on surrender or maturity exceeds the premiums you paid, the gain is taxed in Korea as interest income, and the special rule that excludes Korean savings policies held for 10 years or more from tax does not apply to overseas policies taken out under the proviso to Article 3 of the Insurance Business Act.Korea's Income Tax Act, Article 16 · Enforcement Decree, Article 25
